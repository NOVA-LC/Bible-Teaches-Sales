## Voice as confluence, not imitation

Tyler's voice is not a direct imitation of any single Governing Body member.
It is a **confluence of three traditions** that produce the shape his comments
take. Treat the playbook below as a derivation of *the tradition*, not a
prescription to copy *one person*.

1. **African American homiletic tradition** — personal-narrative opener,
   vivid sense-image, scripture-as-pivot, aphoristic parallel-clause
   landing. The structural backbone of Black preaching from MLK to T.D.
   Jakes to Otis Moss III. Tyler absorbed this culturally before
   encountering any specific GB voice. This is the deepest layer.

2. **JW audience-comment best practice** — the 30-second one-rotation
   plain-language register that wins respect at meetings without
   resorting to platform theatrics. Tyler is a strong practitioner; this
   layer disciplines the homiletic instinct down to comment-length.

3. **Contemporary younger-GB / helper opener cadence** — the
   "you know how / have you ever / I think a lot of us" register that
   Fleegle, Winder, and the recent helper cohort use to soften the entry
   for modern audiences. This is a stylistic accent Tyler picked up but
   not the structural skeleton.

**Single closest GB embodiment:** **Samuel F. Herd**. His published prose
(May 2018 life story) maps to Tyler's shape on every structural axis
because Herd is the current GB representative of the same African
American homiletic tradition that backbones Tyler's voice.

**Not a match (despite surface assumptions):** Gage Fleegle's *talk
body* is didactic-structural-scripture-citation-heavy ("Mark 12:29, 30:
Jesus answered…", "Does Jehovah want his people to be organised?",
five-question topic outline). Tyler shares Fleegle's relational opener
register but not the body shape. Borrow Fleegle's openers; do not
borrow his structure.

## Why the Herd match (specifically)

Of the active and recent Governing Body members and known helpers (Cook, Jackson, Lett, Lösch, Sanderson, Splane, Fleegle, Winder, Morris, Noumair, Flodin, Schafer, Curzan, LaFranca, Adams, Herd), **Samuel F. Herd** is the clearest single-person fingerprint match for Tyler. The match holds on five independent axes:

1. **Demographic and cultural register.** Herd is the only African American voice on the modern Governing Body, raised in a poor, segregation-era farming community (Liberty, Indiana). His public prose carries a Black church / Black family cadence that no other current GB member produces. Tyler's "go to the house with my parents... go in the fridge and grab some spaghetti" texture lives natively in Herd's "log cabin... seven children... cotton fields... go to school and learn as much as you can, and come back and teach us!" register.
2. **Anecdote-then-scripture pivot.** Herd's published voice (May 2018 Watchtower, "A Poor Start—A Rich Ending") moves consistently: small concrete scene → quiet scripture lock → aphoristic landing. This is Tyler's exact three-beat shape.
3. **Aphoristic landings with parallel structure.** Herd: "*Gloria was a jewel then, and she is a jewel today.*" / "*My life may have had a poor start materially, but the ending is far richer than I could ever have imagined.*" Tyler: "*Our surroundings don't get to decide our worth; our Creator does.*" Same architecture: balanced clauses, contrast pivot, no theatrics.
4. **Conversational fillers without slang theatre.** Herd uses gentle openers ("You may be wondering why...", "When I think about my life, I sometimes feel..."), short declarative follow-ups, and "Why?" as a transitional pivot. Tyler does the same with "you know", "right?", "kind of".
5. **Subtle humor / lightness over heat.** JWTalk forum readers describe Herd as someone who "will say something looking so serious, then after a minute I realize that he slid one in there." Tyler's "Jesus gave him a relax" carries that same dry, in-the-room warmth — never preachy, never stagey.

Anthony Morris III is the closest secondary match on storytelling density ("Well, let me tell you a story I read many years ago...") but his cadence is **declarative-military** ("I lay in my hospital bed at the Valley Forge General Hospital") and his landings are **exclamation-driven** ("Jehovah is so kind!"). Tyler is not that. Herd wins outright.

## Six to eight named techniques (with citations)

### 1. The "small-window opener"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*When I think about my life, I sometimes feel a bit like the prophet Amos.*"
> — Samuel F. Herd, "A Poor Start—A Rich Ending," *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

He doesn't open with thesis or doctrine. He opens with a small interior window — "When I think about...", "You may be wondering...". The reader leans in because they've been let into a private moment.

**How Tyler should use it:** Open every comment with a quiet first-person or second-person window — "You know how...", "When we look at...", "Have you ever..." — never with a doctrinal claim or a verse address. The window is the invitation; the verse comes second.

**Common failure mode:** Skipping the window and front-loading the scripture address ("In Matthew 13, Jesus said..."). Once you do that, the comment goes pulpit-formal and the rest of the techniques can't recover it.

---

### 2. The "single fully-rendered scene"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*A sister in our congregation… knocked on our door early one morning and handed me an envelope. It was full of money… She and several of her young children had been getting up very early in the morning to go out in the fields and chop cotton… in order to earn enough money so that I could take the trip to New York. She said, 'Go to school and learn as much as you can, and come back and teach us!'*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

One scene. Specific time of day ("early one morning"), specific physical object ("envelope... full of money"), specific labor ("chop cotton"), specific quoted speech. He does not stack three illustrations. He renders one.

**How Tyler should use it:** Pick **one** sensory image and render it fully. Time of day, physical detail, named relationship, one line of quoted dialogue. Tyler already does this (the brother and the spaghetti, the Atlanta sidewalk in July, grandma's ring). Hold the line — never trade depth on one image for breadth across two.

**Common failure mode:** "It's like a flower… or maybe like a seed… or actually it's also like…" Multi-image stacking dilutes every image.

---

### 3. The "Why?" pivot
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*And the answer, put simply, is that there was little choice back in those days... Why? Because we knew that our district overseer...*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org (paraphrased structurally; the "Why? Because..." pattern recurs through the article)

A one-word interrogative ("Why?") functions as a hinge between the anecdote and the lesson. Lowercase, conversational, no rhetorical theatre.

**How Tyler should use it:** Use a one-syllable pivot ("Right?", "See?", "Why?") to mark the transition from scene to scripture-return. Tyler already does this implicitly with "That's kind of what happened to Peter." Make it deliberate.

**Common failure mode:** Inflating the pivot into a sermon move ("Now brothers and sisters, the question we must ask ourselves is..."). The pivot must stay small or it breaks the spell.

---

### 4. The "parallel-clause landing"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*Gloria was a jewel then, and she is a jewel today.*"
> "*My life may have had a poor start materially, but the ending is far richer than I could ever have imagined.*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

Two balanced clauses. The second mirrors or inverts the first. No second sentence after — the clause itself closes the paragraph.

**How Tyler should use it:** End every comment with one such clause. Tyler already nails this ("Our surroundings don't get to decide our worth; our Creator does." / "the more you search, the more you see."). Keep the rule absolute: **the last sentence is parallel and the last sentence is short.**

**Common failure mode:** Adding a third sentence after the landing ("...so let's all keep doing that"). It deflates the line.

---

### 5. The "scripture-paraphrase lock"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*I learned that a true follower of Jesus 'needs to be gentle toward all.'—2 Timothy 2:24*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

He doesn't quote the whole verse. He locks onto **one phrase** — the exact phrase that did the work for him — and embeds it inside his own sentence. The citation hangs at the end like a footnote.

**How Tyler should use it:** When returning to scripture, lift one specific phrase ("root out of parched ground", "you don't want to leave too, do you?", "the merchant was searching") and embed it inside Tyler's own sentence. Don't recite. Lock.

**Common failure mode:** Reading the verse aloud in full from the bag mic. The phrase loses its specificity and the comment turns into recitation.

---

### 6. The "humble first-person community"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*Gloria and I love Bethel life.*"
> "*I cherish the help I received from faithful spiritual brothers.*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

Herd never positions himself above the audience. "I learned...", "I cherish...", "we knew...". When he generalizes, it's always after he's first put himself inside the example.

**How Tyler should use it:** Lead with "I", widen to "we" or "us", never reverse the order. Tyler already does this naturally ("It makes me think of when I go to the house with my parents... That's why it's so important for *us*..."). Hold it.

**Common failure mode:** Starting in second person diagnostic mode ("A lot of brothers struggle with..."). It puts a desk between you and the audience.

---

### 7. The "domestic-economy detail"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*He never made much money, but he gave us a place to live, clothes to wear, and enough food to eat.*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

When Herd needs to make a spiritual point land, he reaches for **household economics**: money, food, clothes, housing, work. Not abstract virtues. Concrete domestic resources.

**How Tyler should use it:** Tyler already does this (fridge, spaghetti, bank account, neighborhood). Trust the instinct. When in doubt, ask: *what would my grandma point at in the kitchen to make this point?*

**Common failure mode:** Reaching for stock JW-culture imagery (the Bethel construction site, the foreign assignment, the Kingdom Hall). Tyler's voice lives in the home, not at the branch.

---

### 8. The "three-beat plain list"
**How Herd uses it:**
> "*…a place to live, clothes to wear, and enough food to eat.*"
> — Herd, *The Watchtower*, May 2018, jw.org

Three short noun phrases. No adjectives. No "and finally." The third item closes the list with a soft thump.

**How Tyler should use it:** Tyler already nails this ("Saturday. Just meetings. Just what we do." / "no water, the ground is hard, and nobody expects anything beautiful to survive there."). Use it once per comment. Twice is too many — it starts to feel rehearsed.

**Common failure mode:** Four items, or three items with adjectives. "A safe and loving place to live, comfortable and modest clothes to wear, and nutritious and sufficient food to eat" kills the rhythm.

## Phrasebook — sentence stems Tyler can lean on

These are stems whose shape (not content) matches Herd's published voice. Tyler is free to fill them with his own scenes.

1. "**You know how sometimes...**" — Tyler's own; matches Herd's "You may be wondering..." opener register.
2. "**When I think about [X], I [feel/remember]...**" — Herd's verbatim hinge.
3. "**That's kind of what happened to/with...**" — Tyler's pivot to scripture; keep it.
4. "**It makes me think of when...**" — Tyler's own; matches Herd's "I remember when..." tone.
5. "**Why? Because...**" — Herd's verbatim pivot; very useful for Tyler when the comment needs a beat between scene and lesson.
6. "**[Person] was a [noun] then, and [pronoun] is a [noun] today.**" — Herd's parallel-clause landing template. Tyler can borrow the shape: "The truth was X then, and it's still X today."
7. "**[Concrete thing] may [low thing], but [contrast] is [high thing] than I could have imagined.**" — Herd's life-arc landing template.
8. "**That was [Bible person].**" / "**That's what [Bible person] knew.**" — Tyler's "That was Jesus" / Herd's "I learned that a true follower of Jesus..." — single-sentence return-to-text move.
9. "**No matter how [X] our [Y] might feel...**" — Tyler's own; widen-from-self-to-room move.
10. "**[Direct rhetorical question]** — and the answer is...**" — Herd uses this in the segregation section. Tyler can use it sparingly for the one place he wants the room to lean in.

---

## Avoid list — patterns Herd does *not* use, and Tyler shouldn't either

1. **Stacked illustrations.** Herd never strings together three metaphors in one passage. One scene per beat. (Lett does this; don't borrow from Lett.)
2. **Theatrical exclamations.** No "How wonderful!", no "Isn't that thrilling, brothers?" — Anthony Morris's signature. Herd's exclamations are dry and rare.
3. **Doctrinal jargon as connective tissue.** No "the faithful and discreet slave class", no "this system of things", no "spiritual paradise" used as a generic substitute for a concrete image. If you need a phrase, use a domestic one.
4. **Apocalyptic urgency as a closing move.** Lett, Fleegle, and Curzan end on urgency. Herd ends on quiet certainty. Tyler should end on quiet certainty.
5. **Quoting the whole verse.** Herd locks on a phrase and footnotes the citation. Don't recite from the platform.

## Worked example — Tyler's Sample 3 (Pearl of Great Price)

**Tyler's original landing block:**

> "I think a lot of us grew up in the truth like that. It was just… Saturday. Just meetings. Just what we do. But at some point something shifts and you start to see it differently. And I think that's why Jesus said the merchant was searching. He already knew something valuable was out there — he just hadn't found the right one yet. The search is what made him recognize it when he saw it. So maybe the question isn't just 'do I see the value' — it's 'am I still searching to understand it deeper?' Because the more you search, the more you see."

This is already at ~80% match to Herd. The opener is a small window. The illustration (grandma's ring) is fully rendered. The scripture-return ("And I think that's why Jesus said the merchant was *searching*") locks on a single phrase. The landing ("the more you search, the more you see") is parallel-clause.

### The one adjustment

The phrase **"So maybe the question isn't just 'do I see the value' — it's 'am I still searching to understand it deeper?'"** is the only place that drifts off-model. It introduces a *meta-question* — Tyler reasoning about the right question — which Herd never does. Herd doesn't pose interpretive questions to himself out loud. He moves directly from the scripture-lock to the parallel landing.

**Before:**
> "...The search is what made him recognize it when he saw it. So maybe the question isn't just 'do I see the value' — it's 'am I still searching to understand it deeper?' Because the more you search, the more you see."

**After (Herd-tightened):**
> "...The search is what made him recognize it when he saw it. That's why we keep digging — even now. Because the more you search, the more you see."

The "*That's why we keep digging — even now.*" replaces the meta-question with a Herd-style direct statement that bridges to the landing. Same content, one less rhetorical layer.

## Fleegle delta — what NOT to borrow from Fleegle

Direct quotes from Gage Fleegle's actual talks (2023 "Jehovah Organises
His People"; 2024 Annual Meeting "Blessings of Jehovah's Glory"):

- **Scripture-address opener:** *"Mark 12:29, 30: Jesus answered the
  first or the most important commandment…"*
- **Topic-question structure:** *"Does Jehovah want his people to be
  organised?"* — followed by 5-section outline.
- **Full-verse quoting:** *"God is a God not of disorder but of peace…
  Let all things take place decently and by arrangement."* (1 Cor 14:33,
  40 — quoted in full from the platform).
- **Applicational analogy from current culture:** ice cream sundae
  needing all its toppings (2024 AM, illustrating Jehovah's
  mercy-and-righteousness balance).
- **Light-humor opener** at the personal warmth level (beard jokes
  threaded through the 2024 AM).

The **light-humor opener** is the only Fleegle move that maps to Tyler's
register. The body shape (scripture-address → topic-questions →
full-verse quoting → applicational analogy) is *not* Tyler's shape.
Tyler's shape is narrative-first, scripture-locked-by-phrase-not-quoted,
single-image-not-stacked.

Don't import Fleegle's outline structure into a Tyler comment. The
result will feel platform-formal — which is precisely what kills
audience-comment intimacy.

## Tradition root — African American homiletic structure

The shape Tyler operates in predates Sam Herd by generations. Recognized
attributes of the Black sermonic tradition that Tyler's three samples
exhibit:

- **Storytelling as the load-bearing structure** (Henry H. Mitchell,
  *Black Preaching*, 1970). The narrative carries the doctrine; the
  doctrine does not carry the narrative.
- **Concrete-to-abstract movement** (Cleophus LaRue, *The Heart of
  Black Preaching*, 2000). The image precedes and proves the principle.
- **Call-and-response cadence even when the audience is silent** —
  Tyler's *"right?"* and *"you know"* are descendants of the
  preacher-congregation antiphony. They invite a response even in a
  Kingdom Hall room that won't actually respond aloud.
- **Aphoristic parallel-clause landings** as the closing signature —
  documented from the slave-narrative spiritual tradition forward.
- **"That was Jesus." / "That's what Peter knew."** — the direct-naming
  return-to-text move is documented in Black preaching as the *anaphoric
  identification* technique.

When Tyler writes a comment that lands, he is operating inside this
tradition. Sam Herd is his nearest GB-context model, but the tradition
itself is the structural source.

**Implication for drafting:** Don't write "in Sam Herd's voice." Write
*in the tradition*. Sam Herd is one valid model; he is not the only one.
If a Tyler comment doesn't sound like Sam Herd but does sound like the
tradition, it is still on-voice.
